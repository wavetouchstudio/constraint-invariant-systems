*A Control-Theoretic Account of Misinterpreted Clarification and Runaway
Interaction Effects*

# Abstract

“Frames Colliding at Full Speed”

VI.04 traced an asymmetry in how much scrutiny a claim receives
depending on which frame it's read in; this paper zooms into a single
live channel where two frames collide directly and watches the collision
escalate on its own. It analyzes a recurring communication failure in
mixed-context social environments: when a speaker preserves *frame
integrity* through functional clarification, listeners operating under
default social frames may interpret repeated clarification as dominance,
aggression, or hostility. Over time, this misinterpretation can generate
runaway social escalation—resentment, exclusion, and the attribution of
negative character traits to the speaker. The phenomenon is formalised
as a frame-control collision between epistemic and social norms, its
recursive dynamics are described, and the disproportionate impact on
high-precision or atypical communication profiles (including many
autistic speakers) is explained. The paper concludes with implications
for discourse design, group dynamics, and institutional communication.

## Introduction

Conversation relies on implicit agreements about the *type* of activity
occurring, and these agreements are rarely explicit—they are inferred
from tone, convention, and social norms. Problems arise when
participants operate under different framing assumptions: one party
treats language as a *functional instrument* for modeling mechanisms,
while others treat language as a *social instrument* for managing
identity, status, and affiliation. The failure mode this paper examines
is that when a speaker clarifies their own intended frame in response to
misinterpretation, that clarification itself can be interpreted as a
social threat, leading to recursive escalation—a pattern often
mislabeled as rudeness, intensity, condescension, or bullying, despite
the speaker's consistent good-faith intent.

## Frames as Control Structures

### Epistemic vs. Social Frames

| Feature | Epistemic (Functional) Frame | Social (Normative) Frame |
|----|----|----|
| Primary goal | Encode mechanisms, patterns, causal relations. | Manage identity, alignment, hierarchy. |
| Meaning source | Explicitly defined by the speaker. | Inferred from shared norms. |
| Corrections | Restore accuracy of a model. | Read as evaluation of people. |
| Ambiguity | Treated as an error state. | Tolerated to preserve harmony. |
| Default in daily talk | Rarely adopted. | Dominant. |

> Key insight: most everyday conversations default to the social frame;
> functional speakers are exceptions.

### Frame Integrity

*Definition:* the speaker's effort to preserve the original meaning and
scope of their statement against reinterpretation. Its components are:

1.  **Functional definition of terms.**

2.  **Rejection of moral or identity-based readings.**

3.  **Post-misinterpretation clarification** that restores the intended
    domain.

4.  **Maintenance of the original model** rather than adaptation to
    social expectations.

*Role:* a stability strategy in epistemic communication.

## The Misinterpretation Mechanism

| Stage | Event | Interpretation by Listener | Outcome |
|----|----|----|----|
| 1 | Speaker presents a functional statement. | Listener applies *social* interpretation (identity, intent, or character). | *Frame insertion* without speaker consent. |
| 2 | Speaker clarifies functional intent. | Listener perceives correction of the listener, rejection of shared norms, assertion of interpretive authority. | *Social threat* perceived. |
| 3 | Listener reacts defensively or emotionally. | Speaker must clarify again to preserve meaning. | Emotional load increases; semantic progress stalls. |

*Note:* the content of the clarification matters less than its *pattern*
in the listener's frame.

## Recursive Escalation Dynamics

### Feedback Loop

| Step | Description | Consequence |
|----|----|----|
| A | Functional statement by speaker. | Listener imposes social interpretation. |
| B | Speaker's clarification of frame integrity. | Listener perceives status/ego threat. |
| C | Listener's emotional/defensive response. | Speaker must clarify again. |
| D | Repeated cycle. | Emotional load rises; semantic progress diminishes. |

### Why It Runs Away

The speaker's corrections stay consistent while the listener's social
frame persists, and social systems penalise repeated correction
regardless of accuracy. Over time, evaluation shifts from *what is said*
to *who keeps correcting*—which is the origin of the "runaway" effect.

## Attribution Shift and Social Labeling

Once recursion persists, observers shift from mechanism-based
explanations to character-based attributions:

| Common Labels | Source of Label | Timing |
|----|----|----|
| "Intense" | Repeated functional statements | After several clarifications |
| "Condescending" | Frequent corrections | After a loop of misunderstandings |
| "Hostile" | Perceived social threat | Once recursion escalates |
| "Dominant" | Persistent framing | After a series of social misreads |

*Important:* the speaker's intent and tone may remain unchanged
throughout.

## Disproportionate Impact on High-Precision Communicators

### High-Cognition Profiles

High-cognition communicators think in models or abstractions, treat
ambiguity as error, and prioritize correctness over harmony—which makes
them more likely to maintain frame integrity under pressure, and so more
likely to trigger recursion.

### Autistic Communication Profiles

Autistic communicators often use words literally or functionally, do not
rely on implied social meaning, correct misunderstandings directly, and
resist softening language that changes meaning. Frequently placed in
environments where social frames dominate, this style gets misread as
hostility, with potential bullying or exclusion as a result.
*Conclusion:* the bullying or exclusion originates from *systematic
frame mismatch* combined with social intolerance for repeated
correction, not from autism itself.

## Why This Is Not a Moral Failure

| Perspective | What Happens | Why It Is Structural |
|----|----|----|
| Speaker | Preserves meaning. | Not attempting dominance. |
| Listener | Enforces familiar norms. | Not malicious. |
| Observers | Pattern-match behaviour to heuristics. | Not cruel. |
| Social System | Optimised for harmony, not epistemic stability. | Treats precision as threat. |

## Implications

| Domain | Key Take-aways | Practical Steps |
|----|----|----|
| Individuals | Persistent clarification carries social cost. | Use *frame surrender* or *selective disengagement* to reduce cost; avoid over-clarification in mixed-frame settings. |
| Groups & Institutions | Norms penalising correction harm precise communicators. | Explicitly allow frame clarification without penalty; educate members on frame dynamics. |
| AI & Mediated Communication | Language systems should distinguish clarification from dominance. | Conversational agents model frame mismatch; flag potential escalation early. |

## Concluding Analysis

When speakers clarify their own frame to preserve meaning, and listeners
reinterpret that clarification through social norms, a recursive
escalation can emerge. Over time, the pattern is mislabeled as
aggression or bullying, even though it originates in good-faith
communication. This dynamic explains why autistic individuals and other
high-precision communicators are frequently targeted or excluded—not
because of what they say, but because they must continually defend their
frame in environments that treat such defense as a social violation.
Understanding this mechanism reframes the issue from one of personality
or pathology to one of incompatible control systems operating in the
same conversational space.

### Key Contributions

1.  Formalisation of the frame-control collision between epistemic and
    social norms.

2.  Recursive dynamics and their escalation mechanism.

3.  Identification of high-precision and autistic profiles as
    disproportionately impacted.

4.  Practical guidelines for reducing escalation in individual, group,
    and AI-mediated communication.

# Conclusion

“Frames Colliding at Full Speed”

This paper presents a control-theoretic account of how repeated
clarification—intended to preserve epistemic accuracy—can trigger social
escalation when listeners operate under default harmony-oriented norms.
The contribution of Frame Integrity and Recursive Social Escalation is
therefore a structured account of its governing constraints, mechanisms,
and failure conditions. Its claims remain bounded by the evidence and
counterexamples specified in the paper, and its practical value depends
on whether the proposed relationships improve explanation, prediction,
or correction in the relevant domain. The next paper (VI.06) moves from
social frame collisions to the language itself, asking what gets lost
whenever meaning is compiled down into words at all.
