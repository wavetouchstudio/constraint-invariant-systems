# Abstract

“Finding the Same Skeleton in Different Bodies”

II.05 treated symbolic frames as translations against a stable
constraint node; this paper turns that same move into a working method.
It formalizes a reasoning technique that treats domain-specific jargon
and disciplinary gatekeeping not as obstacles but as compressed
structural constraints. By internalizing these constraints and
redeploying them outside their native silos without altering their
functional behavior, the method exposes invariant system shapes masked
by incentive-driven semantic partitioning.

This is not interdisciplinarity in the analogy-based sense—analogy
imports conclusions from one domain to another without preserving
internal legitimacy. Instead, Constraint-Leveraged Recombination
preserves constraint integrity while discarding disciplinary narrative,
and it is falsifiable through structural stress-testing rather than
rhetorical appeal.

The technique is especially effective in mature knowledge systems
characterized by abstraction duplication and siloed meaning—precisely
the conditions described in II.04's analysis of cognitive stratification
where information compression substitutes for reciprocity across domains
that share underlying structures but have forgotten they do so.

## **1. The Structural Problem**

Mature knowledge systems exhibit predictable dynamics: increasing
semantic specialization, locally optimized vocabularies, incentives
favoring novelty over unification, and boundary enforcement discouraging
equivalence.

The result is siloed meaning—parallel abstractions describing identical
structures under different names. Traditional synthesis fails because it
relies on analogy rather than structural necessity, lacks internal
legitimacy within each discipline, and triggers defensive boundary
reactions when gatekeepers perceive external intrusion as illegitimate.

## **2. Jargon as Compressed Constraint**

Durable jargon is not ornamental; it encodes stabilized structural
pressures that have survived disciplinary testing. Each stable term
functions simultaneously as a compression artifact, a boundary condition
marker, and a dynamic constraint specification.

Examples across fields include feedback, hysteresis, attractors, path
dependence, and collapse modes—all of which describe structurally
equivalent phenomena despite operating in different vocabularies. The
method treats jargon as portable structure rather than domain-owned
language.

## **3. Gatekeeping as Verification**

Gatekeeping mechanisms enforce correct usage, internal consistency, and
constraint preservation—functions that are verification rather than
obstruction when viewed from the right angle. Redeployment is permitted
only if core constraints remain intact, failure modes are preserved, and
predictive behavior survives stress.

Gatekeeping thus becomes a structural test harness: each discipline's
scrutiny acts as independent validation of whether the constraint
structure holds under relocation rather than testing whether surface
meaning aligns with disciplinary identity.

## **4. The Core Technique**

The method proceeds in six steps. Blank-state initialization suspends
analogy and disciplinary loyalty while minimizing narrative bias—this is
where most attempts fail, because the practitioner has not yet cleared
their internal model of prior associations.

### **Step 2 — Deep Internalization**

Master the jargon precisely enough to understand encoded assumptions,
boundary conditions, and what breaks under misuse. This is not
superficial familiarity; it requires understanding why each term exists
as a constraint specification rather than decorative terminology.

### **Step 3 — Constraint Extraction**

Remove disciplinary narrative while preserving structural role, dynamic
behavior, and limit conditions. The discipline's story is discarded; its
structure remains.

### **Step 4 — Isomorphic Mapping**

Identify other domains exhibiting identical constraint topology,
feedback structure, and limit behavior—mapping based on system shape
rather than surface similarity. This is where the method diverges from
analogy: it seeks structural equivalence that survives disciplinary
translation.

### **Step 5 — Reinstantiation**

Redeploy the term without redefining it—the constraint carries its own
meaning once properly extracted. The discipline's ownership claim
dissolves; what remains is the structure itself.

### **Step 6 — Structural Stress Test**

If constraints fail, discard the mapping. If they hold, an invariant has
been exposed—this binary outcome is what makes the method falsifiable
rather than merely suggestive.

## **5. Distinction from Equivocation**

The technique is frequently mistaken for semantic sliding, but the
distinction is structural: equivocation shifts meaning while dropping
constraints and importing conclusions; Constraint-Leveraged
Recombination preserves constraints, discards surface narrative, and
forces structural behavior to re-emerge.

The method is conservative toward structure and indifferent toward
semantic ownership—this indifference is what earns disciplinary
suspicion in the first place.

## **6. Incentive Independence**

The technique requires relative independence from publication pressures,
funding incentives tied to novelty, and reputational penalties for
unification—without this independence, abstraction duplication is
rewarded and invariant collapse is discouraged.

This explains why the method is rare inside institutions and more common
at disciplinary margins: institutional incentive structures actively
punish what it would take to perform Constraint-Leveraged Recombination
properly.

## **7. Why It Produces Insight**

Novelty emerges through recompression rather than invention—the same
constraint structure becomes visible from a new angle because the
disciplinary framing has been stripped away.

The technique reveals hidden invariants, redundant abstractions,
misattributed domain specificity, and structural equivalence masked by
vocabulary. Insights often appear "obvious in hindsight" because
constraint alignment makes them inevitable once the right perspective is
held.

## **8. Risks and Failure Modes**

Identifiable risks include false isomorphism from shallow mastery,
social friction across jurisdictional boundaries, and overextension
beyond constraint limits—each of which produces distinct failure
signatures that can be recognized and addressed.

### **Mitigation**

Mitigation requires deep technical fluency in each discipline being
compared, willingness to discard failed mappings without defending them
as "attempts," and refusal to moralize disagreement between disciplinary
communities.

## **9. Scope and Applicability**

The method is most effective in late-stage scientific fields,
incentive-stressed institutions, complex adaptive systems, and
high-abstraction-density environments—precisely where abstraction
duplication has accumulated to the point where structural equivalence
becomes visible.

### **Less Effective In**

The method is less effective in early exploratory domains, empirically
sparse fields, and highly context-dependent interpretive
disciplines—where constraint structures have not yet stabilized enough
to be extracted portably.

## **10. Falsifiability**

The method fails if constraint preservation cannot be demonstrated,
redeployed jargon loses explanatory force, mappings rely on analogy
rather than structural necessity, or insights collapse under adversarial
scrutiny.

## Concluding Analysis

Constraint-Leveraged Recombination reframes disciplinary gatekeeping as
structural leverage. By treating jargon as compressed constraint rather
than institutional property, it enables cross-domain synthesis without
sacrificing rigor—this is not interdisciplinarity but structural reuse
under constraint.

# Conclusion

“Finding the Same Skeleton in Different Bodies”

This paper introduces a reasoning method that treats jargon as
compressed structural constraints rather than semantic decoration. The
contribution of Constraint-Leveraged Recombination is therefore a
structured account of its governing constraints, mechanisms, and failure
conditions. Its claims remain bounded by the evidence and
counterexamples specified in the paper, and its practical value depends
on whether the proposed relationships improve explanation, prediction,
or correction in the relevant domain. The next paper (II.07) applies
this same cross-domain move to a concrete case: why technological
scaling laws break down once you stop treating them as a single-layer
property.
