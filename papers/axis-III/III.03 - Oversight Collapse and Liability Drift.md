# Abstract

Legal exposure in informal public systems and epistemic chilling in
contemporary oversight share a common structural failure mode: the
transformation of moral engagement into liability or censorious
scrutiny. Legal Centrality Drift shows how high‑integrity actors—those
who are visible, competent, and document their interactions—are
reinterpreted by legal systems as operational supervisors, thereby
accruing disproportionate risk. Epistemic Chilling, modeled as pipeline
attribution, demonstrates how institutions treat deep inquiry into
controversial domains as evidence of endorsement, creating a gradient of
risk that deters precisely the insight needed to mitigate harm. This
paper integrates the two frameworks, identifies shared
mechanisms—image‑driven legitimacy, context collapse, and asymmetric
risk allocation—and proposes design‑level interventions that restore
analytic capacity without abandoning safety constraints.

# 1. INTRODUCTION

Modern institutions balance innovation against risk prevention.

In informal public arenas, moral leaders become exposed when their
actions are legally construed as facilitation or supervision.

In formal oversight, researchers who probe deep into controversial
domains are perceived as participants rather than analysts, a phenomenon
called epistemic chilling.

Both contexts arise from structural asymmetries that fail to distinguish
intent from operational control.

# 3. MECHANISMS OF EXPOSURE AND CHILLING

## 3.1 Legal Exposure Pathways

# 1. Visibility – Public or recorded interactions create timestamps and association evidence.

# 2. Documentation Paradox – Objections may demonstrate non‑approval but also prove knowledge.

# 3. Timing Windows – Early involvement low risk; continued engagement increases inference of participation.

# 4. Online Amplification – Public content defeats ignorance claims and establishes searchable timelines.

## 3.2 Epistemic Chilling Pathways

# 1. Gradient of Scrutiny – From citations to primary research, risk escalates non‑linearly.

# 2. Pre‑emptive Cognitive Modeling – Institutions assess imagined misuse rather than present analytical merit.

# 3. Context Collapse – Critical distance flattened into coarse signals, conflating analysis with endorsement.

# 4. Recursive Oversight Instability – Watchdogs’ privileged access requires monitoring, creating a feedback loop of performative compliance.

## 3.3 Common Structural Drivers

Legibility vs. Intent: Systems prefer clear signals over nuanced
motivation.

Image Preservation: Reputation often outweighs substantive change.

Resource Constraints: Limited capacity forces shortcutting to visible
compliance.

# 5. STRUCTURAL IMPACTS

Image Supremacy: Legibility of actions eclipses ethical intent, creating
a safety‑first bias.

Safety vs. Innovation Tension: Over‑regulation protects reputations but
stifles essential knowledge.

Reputational Contagion: Legal exposure spills into civil, professional,
and employment domains, extending risk beyond criminal courts.

Oversight Fragility: Recursive watchdog dynamics generate oscillations
between over‑chilling and backlash, degrading detection accuracy.

## Concluding Analysis

Legal exposure and epistemic chilling both arise from systems that
prioritize legibility and image over nuanced intent. The result is a
structural bias that penalizes the very actors—HSAs and analysts—who
possess the competence and visibility necessary to identify and mitigate
harm. By mapping the shared mechanisms, we see that interventions
effective in one domain (e.g., liability shields) can inform solutions
in the other. The challenge is to calibrate oversight and legal
interpretation so that moral engagement is protected while still
preventing misuse, restoring a balance between safety and innovation.

Asymmetric Risk Allocation – Rewarding superficial compliance while

Documentation Paradox – Objections demonstrate non‑approval but also
prove knowledge.
