# Abstract

“When No One’s Actually in Charge Anymore”

This opening paper of Axis III integrates two inter-related examinations
of how contemporary societies cope with conflict when traditional human
coordination or legal enforcement mechanisms become ineffective.
*Automated Mediation in Bottlenecked Public Spaces* argues that
high-density environments (shopping aisles, airports, traffic
intersections, workplaces, online forums) routinely collapse into
dominance signaling, narrative sabotage, and bystander withdrawal; when
the speed, consistency, and impartiality of human arbiters cannot be
guaranteed, societies outsource coordination to algorithmic arbitrators,
producing a form of "externalized morality" in which machines enforce
rules neutrally at the expense of informal social norms. *Hollow
Authority and the Asymmetry of Self-Defense Doctrine* examines the legal
doctrine of private self-defense in liberal democracies, which
constrains lawful defenders to stringent standards of imminence,
necessity, and proportionality while addressing criminal violence only
after the fact; when state enforcement is delayed or ineffective, the
doctrine shifts risk onto victims and enables criminals to exploit the
system's asymmetry, creating a paradoxical increase in catastrophic
violence, and the drift toward normative absolutism tightens restraints
without addressing enforcement deficits, deepening the legitimacy gap.

Together these studies illuminate a systemic pattern: when the capacity
of human or institutional actors to manage conflict erodes, societies
compensate by institutionalizing neutral, algorithmic mediation or by
reinforcing doctrinal restraint, and each compensation introduces new
vulnerabilities of its own.

## 1. Background and Context

Spatial constraints force high interaction density in retail aisles,
airports, intersections, workplaces, and online platforms, and classical
game-theoretic bottleneck models predict that this density escalates
into dominance signaling and narrative sabotage. Liberal legal systems
separately privilege minimization of irreversible harm by authorized
actors, with doctrine relying on the assumption that rapid, credible
state intervention offsets private defensive actions.

Both domains share a common structural feature: a failure to align
enforcement, human or institutional, with the pace of conflict. The
result in each case is a systemic asymmetry that re-routes control to
neutral or restricted mechanisms.

## 2. Theoretical Framework

Authority — the right to use force or mediate — becomes detached from
the process that actually enforces it, whether that process is human
discretion or algorithmic rule-application. This decoupling produces
what this paper calls hollow authority: high procedural legitimacy
paired with low practical deterrence, strong constraints on lawful
actors, and weak real-time constraints on unlawful ones.

Where shared moral etiquette fails, societies outsource normative
enforcement to machines instead — externalized morality. The predictable
consequence is an asymmetry of risk: victims bear higher risk due to
constrained defensive options, while criminals benefit from delayed
repercussions.

## 3. Automated Mediation in Bottlenecked Public Spaces

Bottleneck dynamics follow a predictable escalation loop: dominance
signaling triggers escalation of dominance, which produces narrative
sabotage, then bystander withdrawal, and finally etiquette collapse.
Ambiguity, weak enforcement, scale, and temporal constraints are the
conditions under which this loop reliably completes.

Automation functions as a neutral arbitrator because it offers
non-negotiable legitimacy (decisions are algorithmic, not subject to
bargaining), impersonality (no ego or social identity signals),
immutability to provocation (unaffected by image games), and predictable
enforcement (uniform, transparent rule application) — achieved through
rule-based arbitration, immediate feedback, and uniform behavior.

Self-driving shopping carts illustrate the pattern concretely: a
functional architecture of aisle navigation, predictive routing,
explicit yielding rules, and uniform speed and spacing reduces dominance
signaling and prevents narrative sabotage, and adoption correlates with
high-density retail environments even where it draws symbolic resistance
framed as dehumanization.

The same substitution recurs across domains. Autonomous vehicles and
smart intersections replace traffic bias, inconsistency, and slow human
response with predictable, impartial right-of-way. Automated boarding
and security lanes replace intent interpretation and argumentation with
uniform, rapid processing. Workflow and ticketing systems replace
weaponized discretion and favoritism with transparent, rule-based
allocation. Algorithmic moderation and rate limiting replace narrative
framing and bias with consistent policy enforcement.

None of this is free of cost. Embedded prejudice can enter through the
rules themselves, requiring regular audits and diverse data sets; edge
cases get mishandled without exception-handling protocols; informal
human kindness erodes without hybrid systems that preserve oversight;
and opacity persists without explainable-AI techniques. Machines
circumvent the need for functional social norms by providing
deterministic arbitration, but resilience still depends on the system's
ability to hold stable once norms have failed — with consequences for
retail design, smart infrastructure, policy frameworks, organizational
governance, individual user experience, and digital literacy.

## 4. Hollow Authority and the Asymmetry of Self-Defense Doctrine

The core doctrinal asymmetry is structural: authorized force is
constrained before use, while unauthorized force is addressed only after
the fact. Regulation focuses on the actors within the system's reach,
leaving victims disproportionately burdened, on the doctrinal assumption
that rapid, credible state intervention will offset private defensive
action. When that assumption fails, the result is unchecked escalation,
low immediate cost for criminals, and repeated victim exposure — a
symbolic monopoly over force without functional deterrence, expanded
hollow authority in practice.

The behavioral effect is a paradox: criminals perceive lower risk while
law-abiding citizens face higher perceived risk, so those with the most
to lose are the most constrained, and constraint produces hesitation.
Small violations accumulate under this pressure-cooker effect,
compressing into decisive moments that lead to catastrophic escalation,
even as empirical data on the pattern remains mixed and policy discourse
often discounts evidence that conflicts with dominant moral narratives.

The doctrine drifts toward absolutism in response: moral asymmetry is
treated as a first principle, with authorized violence viewed as
uniquely corrosive; error aversion and a zero-mistake ideal tighten
constraints in response to misuse evidence; context sensitivity
collapses, flattening the distinction between home intrusion and public
encounters; and absolutism strengthens precisely as public protection
weakens, creating a self-sealing loop in which harm becomes the
justification for more restraint. The long-term costs are declining
compliance, loss of moral credibility, increased self-solving of
disputes, and episodic catastrophic failure — driven by normative
commitments that prioritize moral purity over adaptive fit.

## 5. Comparative Analysis and Thematic Convergence

Both domains share the same underlying theme: bottlenecked interactions
produce dominance signaling and escalation, legal enforcement produces a
doctrine of restraint, and both respond by decoupling authority from
effective mediation. The trade-off differs only in currency —
public-space automation trades informal norms for neutral, rule-based
order, while legal restraint trades defensive liberty for procedural
safety, shifting risk onto victims either way.

The common vulnerabilities follow the same shape: public space loses its
informal checks because escalation outruns human capacity; legal
doctrine produces hollow enforcement because state deterrence fails;
automation embeds bias rooted in human prejudice; and doctrine tightens
into absolutism to defend its own institutional legitimacy.

## 6. Key Terms

Dominance signaling names behaviors intended to assert control in a
shared space; narrative sabotage, the manipulation of shared
expectations to undermine cooperation; a bottlenecked public space, an
environment where spatial or resource constraints cause high interaction
density; a neutral arbitrator, an enforcer that applies rules
impartially, free from social identity; externalized morality, the
transfer of normative enforcement from human norms to machine
arbitration; hollow authority, a claim of monopoly over legitimate force
with ineffective deterrence; self-defense doctrine, the legal framework
restricting private defensive force to conditions of imminence,
necessity, and proportionality; and absolutism, the normative rigidity
that tightens restraints in response to enforcement gaps.

## 7. Policy, Institutional, and Individual Implications

Policy development calls for regulatory standards governing algorithmic
arbitration — transparency, accountability, auditability — alongside
reform of self-defense law to align restraint with credible public
protection, through adaptive thresholds and context-specific doctrines.
Institutional governance calls for hybrid models that combine
algorithmic neutrality with human discretion for edge cases, plus
strengthened state enforcement capabilities such as rapid-response teams
and predictive policing.

At the individual and collective level, what matters is users'
experience of control versus perceived fairness, the growing imperative
of digital literacy for understanding algorithmic decisions, and victim
risk perception and hesitation dynamics. Long-term stability requires
monitoring legitimacy gaps, building early-warning systems for risk
compression, and investing institutionally in real-time deterrence to
counter hollow authority.

## 8. Conclusions

In both public-space coordination and self-defense law, the decoupling
of authority from effective mediation creates systemic asymmetries.
Automated mediation solves spatial dominance at the cost of informal
norms, potentially eroding social etiquette, while legal restraint
solves the problem of irreversible harm by authorized actors but, when
enforcement is weak, paradoxically increases catastrophic risk. Both
responses are internally coherent, yet both reveal a structural
brittleness that can erode legitimacy and precipitate instability —
future resilience depends on aligning enforcement mechanisms, human or
algorithmic, with the real-time dynamics of conflict, so that neutral
mediation or doctrinal restraint do not simply shift risk elsewhere.

# Conclusion

“When No One’s Actually in Charge Anymore”

This combined paper examines how governance first fails at the level of
real-time coordination when human discretion is placed under asymmetric
risk, time pressure, and legitimacy constraints. The contribution of
Coordination Failure and the Externalization of Authority is therefore a
structured account of its governing constraints, mechanisms, and failure
conditions. Its claims remain bounded by the evidence and
counterexamples specified in the paper, and its practical value depends
on whether the proposed relationships improve explanation, prediction,
or correction in the relevant domain. As the opening paper of Axis III,
this sets the frame the rest of the axis develops: the next paper
(III.02) turns from real-time coordination failure to what a system does
when it must actively eject a process to preserve its own image.
