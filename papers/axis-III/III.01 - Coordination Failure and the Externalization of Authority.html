Abstract

This master document integrates two inter‑related examinations of how contemporary societies cope with conflict when traditional human coordination or legal enforcement mechanisms become ineffective.

1. *Automated Mediation in Bottlenecked Public Spaces* argues that high‑density environments (shopping aisles, airports, traffic intersections, workplaces, online forums) routinely collapse into dominance signaling, narrative sabotage, and bystander withdrawal. When the speed, consistency, and impartiality of human arbiters cannot be guaranteed, societies outsource coordination to algorithmic arbitrators. The result is a form of “externalized morality” in which machines enforce rules neutrally, thereby preserving spatial order at the expense of informal social norms.

2. *Hollow Authority and the Asymmetry of Self‑Defense Doctrine* examines the legal doctrine of private self‑defence in liberal democracies. The doctrine constrains lawful defenders to stringent standards of imminence, necessity, and proportionality while addressing criminal violence only after the fact. When state enforcement is delayed or ineffective, the doctrine shifts risk onto victims and enables criminals to exploit the system’s asymmetry, creating a paradoxical increase in catastrophic violence. The paper further explores the drift toward normative absolutism, which tightens restraints without addressing enforcement deficits, thereby deepening the legitimacy gap.

Together these studies illuminate a systemic pattern: when the capacity of human or institutional actors to manage conflict erodes, societies compensate by institutionalizing neutral, algorithmic mediation or by reinforcing doctrinal restraint, each of which introduces new vulnerabilities.

1. BACKGROUND AND CONTEXT

1.1 Public‑Space Bottlenecks

Spatial constraints that force high interaction density (retail aisles, airports, intersections, workplaces, online platforms).

Classical game‑theoretic bottleneck models predict escalating dominance signaling and narrative sabotage.

1.2 Legal Enforcement of Self‑Defense

Liberal legal systems privilege minimization of irreversible harm by authorised actors.

Doctrine relies on the assumption that rapid, credible state intervention offsets private defensive actions.

1.3 Common Structural Feature

Both domains exhibit a failure to align enforcement (human or institutional) with the pace of conflict.

The result is a systemic asymmetry that re‑routes control to neutral or restricted mechanisms.

2. THEORETICAL FRAMEWORK

2.1 Authority‑Process Decoupling

Authority (the right to use force or mediate) becomes detached from the process that actually enforces it (human discretion or algorithmic rule‑application).

2.2 Hollow Authority

High procedural legitimacy but low practical deterrence.

Strong constraints on lawful actors, weak real‑time constraints on unlawful actors.

2.3 Externalized Morality

Outsourcing normative enforcement to machines when shared moral etiquette fails.

2.4 Asymmetry of Risk

Victims bear higher risk due to constrained defensive options; criminals benefit from delayed repercussions.

4. HOLLOW AUTHORITY AND THE ASYMMETRY OF SELF‑DEFENSE DOCTRINE

4.1 Core Doctrinal Asymmetry

Authorized force constrained pre‑use; unauthorized force addressed post‑use.

Structural pattern: regulation focuses on actors within system’s reach, leaving victims disproportionately burdened.

4.2 Assumption of Effective Public Protection

Doctrine presumes rapid, credible state intervention.

Failure of this premise leads to unchecked escalation, low immediate cost for criminals, and repeated victim exposure.

4.3 Hollow Authority Defined (expanded)

Symbolic monopoly over force without functional deterrence.

4.4 Risk Asymmetry and Behavioral Effects

Criminals perceive lower risk; law‑abiding citizens face higher perceived risk.

Paradox: those with most to lose are most constrained, causing hesitation.

4.5 Pressure‑Cooker Effect

Small violations accumulate, compress into decisive moments, leading to catastrophic escalation.

4.5 Evidence Problem & Narrative Persistence

Empirical data mixed; policy discourse often discounts evidence that conflicts with dominant moral narratives.

4.6 Drift Toward Absolutism

Moral asymmetry as first principle: authorised violence viewed as uniquely corrosive.

Error aversion and zero‑mistake ideal tighten constraints in response to misuse evidence.

Collapse of context sensitivity flattens distinctions (home intrusion vs public encounters).

Absolutism strengthens as public protection weakens, creating a self‑sealing loop: harm → justification for more restraint.

4.5 Implications for Long‑Term Stability

Declining compliance, moral credibility loss, increased self‑solving, episodic catastrophic failure.

4.6 Synthesis

Absolutist drift is driven by normative commitments prioritising moral purity over adaptive fit.

Reform of self‑defence law to align restraint with credible public protection (e.g., adaptive thresholds, context‑specific doctrines).

7.2 Institutional Governance

Hybrid models that combine algorithmic neutrality with human discretion for edge cases.

Institutional investment in real‑time deterrence to counter hollow authority.

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

8. CONCLUSIONS

In both public‑space coordination and self‑defence law, the decoupling of authority from effective mediation creates systemic asymmetries.

Automated mediation solves spatial dominance at the cost of informal norms, potentially eroding social etiquette.

Legal restraint solves the problem of irreversible harm by authorised actors but, when enforcement is weak, paradoxically increases catastrophic risk.

Both responses, while internally coherent, reveal structural brittleness that can erode legitimacy and precipitate instability.

Future resilience depends on aligning enforcement mechanisms—human or algorithmic—with the real‑time dynamics of conflict, ensuring that neutral mediation or doctrinal restraint do not merely shift risk elsewhere.

Conclusion

This combined paper examines how governance first fails at the level of real-time coordination when human discretion is placed under asymmetric risk, time pressure, and legitimacy constraints. The contribution of Coordination Failure and the Externalization of Authority is therefore a structured account of its governing constraints, mechanisms, and failure conditions. Its claims remain bounded by the evidence and counterexamples specified in the paper, and its practical value depends on whether the proposed relationships improve explanation, prediction, or correction in the relevant domain.
