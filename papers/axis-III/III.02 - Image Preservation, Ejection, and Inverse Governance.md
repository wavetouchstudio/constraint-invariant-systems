# Abstract

“Protecting the Brand by Sacrificing the People”

Where III.01 traced coordination failure at the level of real-time
enforcement, this paper turns to what institutions do once they must
actively protect their own image. The two bodies of work presented here
converge on a single structural principle: when institutions are bounded
by finite resources, normative mandates, and reputational thresholds,
they respond to internal pressure by preserving external image rather
than integrating disruptive variation. In diversity, equity, and
inclusion (DEI) and affirmative-action (AA) programs, this manifests as
second-order rules that prioritize metrics and surface compliance,
triggering ejection of dissenting actors. In governance systems more
broadly, the same dynamics appear as compliance-elasticity inversion,
whereby increased enforcement intensity paradoxically reduces
substantive compliance and heightens harm. Together, these analyses
illuminate how authority-process decoupling and image preservation
generate self-reinforcing cycles that erode substantive outcomes,
produce backlash, and ultimately risk institutional collapse. The paper
offers a unified framework — bounded-system dynamics, second-order
rules, and compliance-elasticity — and proposes design interventions
that restore alignment between authority, mediation, and substantive
transformation.

## 1. Introduction

This paper examines DEI and AA programs as bounded systems that exhibit
ejection dynamics, analyzes governance inversion through
compliance-elasticity, and demonstrates that both domains share
identical structural asymmetries. Three shared foundations run through
both: authority-process decoupling, where the right to act is separated
from the capacity to act effectively; image preservation, where
institutions maintain reputation by adhering to surface norms; and
ejection dynamics, the expulsion of elements that threaten equilibrium.

## 2. Theoretical Background

Bounded-system dynamics set the stage: finite resources, norms, and
reputational thresholds limit an institution's tolerance for deviation,
so small deviations are absorbed while larger ones trigger second-order
rules that preserve image instead of resolving the underlying problem.

| Element | Purpose | Outcome | Example |
|----|----|----|----|
| Compliance & reporting | Metric emphasis | Appearing orderly | DEI dashboards |
| Broad commitment statements | Stakeholder satisfaction | Structural inertia | “We are committed to equity” |
| Procedural fixes | Redirect criticism | Limited substantive change | Token training modules |

Authority-process decoupling occurs when authority is granted — the
right to enforce self-defense, or to mandate DEI — without effective
mediation, whether that means enforcement capacity or systemic change;
the result is hollow authority. This paper formalizes the dynamic as the
compliance-elasticity of governance (CEG), the ratio of the change in
substantive compliance (C<sub>s</sub> — alignment with safety, welfare,
fairness) to the change in governance pressure (P<sub>g</sub> —
enforcement intensity, rule density, monitoring scope, penalty severity,
narrative emphasis): CEG = ΔC<sub>s</sub> / ΔP<sub>g</sub>. When CEG is
greater than zero, governance is functional (negative feedback); near
zero, the system has saturated; below zero, governance has inverted into
positive feedback.

## 3. DEI/AA as a Bounded-System Mechanism

DEI programs aim to create inclusive environments reflecting demographic
diversity; AA programs use targeted measures in admissions and hiring to
offset historic disadvantage. Supporters argue these programs correct
systemic discrimination, enhance fairness, and improve performance
through diverse perspectives. Critics counter that they undermine merit
perception and can backfire into negative attitudes among non-target
groups.

Internal pressure on these programs comes from two directions: normative
pressure, where visibility of inequity demands corrective action, and
operational pressure, where implementation costs, resistance, and
conflicting metrics push institutions toward rapid, low-cost solutions.
The second-order rules that emerge in response are familiar —
metric-driven compliance, broad statements of commitment, procedural
fixes rather than structural change — and their consequence is an
emphasis on appearance rather than internal adaptation, avoidance of
hierarchy-threatening change, and marginalization of innovators wherever
integration costs run high.

Ejection dynamics follow directly: marginalization or removal of DEI
leaders, restructuring of DEI officer roles, scaling back of
initiatives, and a rhetorical shift from integration to preservation.
The unintended consequences compound the original problem — token hires
that never achieve full integration, talent diverted away from core
decision-making, and suppression of substantive debate. The design
response this paper proposes is to reframe success metrics toward deep
integration and agency, create low-cost dissent pathways, link DEI to
core performance objectives, and encourage iterative learning.

## 4. Inverse Governance and Compliance-Elasticity

Time lags and hysteresis shape how inversion unfolds: a temporary
compliance boost in the short term gives way to a plateau in the medium
term as adaptation spreads, and to decline in the long term as evasion,
burnout, and legitimacy loss come to dominate. Persistent negative CEG
across multiple time windows is accompanied by rising evasion
sophistication, increasing burnout, and growth in performative
compliance.

| Consequence | Description |
|----|----|
| Systemic burnout | Workers and citizens exhausted by compliance tasks |
| Performative compliance | Surface safety, no real protection |
| Punishment of help-seeking | Risk signalling and exclusion |
| Rights erosion | Abstraction and categorisation |
| Disproportionate harm | Vulnerable populations disproportionately affected |

Negative elasticity emerges reliably when three conditions converge:
evasion capacity exceeds enforcement capacity, legitimacy falls below
threshold, and coordination among evaders becomes feasible. Once all
three hold, a recursive attribution error sets in — visible compliance
rises even as substantive compliance falls, and escalation trains
evasion, expands documentation, narrows narratives, and converts
ambiguity into suspicion. This abstraction produces its own due-process
failure: help-seeking becomes risk signalling, care records become
exclusionary flags, hypothetical reasoning is misread as confession, and
subjective experience is reclassified as pathology.

Institutional blindness compounds all of this through measurement
misalignment, incentive capture (escalation gets rewarded), attribution
error (individuals get blamed instead of structure), and narrative
lock-in, where falling compliance becomes the justification for still
more control. The thermodynamic constraint underneath is that
inefficiency grows linearly while inversion grows exponentially,
eventually demanding more energy than the institution has — the result
is burnout, exit cascades, and collapse.

## 5. Comparative Analysis

DEI/AA and CEG-based governance inversion share the same shape:
authority is granted — as policy in one case, as rule density in the
other — and answered with second-order rules in the DEI case or negative
compliance-elasticity in the governance case, producing hollow authority
and ejection in one domain and inverse governance with performative
compliance in the other.

| Concept | DEI/AA Context | Governance Inversion Context |
|----|----|----|
| Bounded System | Finite resources, norms, reputation | Finite enforcement capacity, legitimacy |
| Authority-Process Decoupling | Mandate DEI without structural change | Rule density without substantive compliance |
| Second-Order Rule | Metric emphasis, procedural fixes | Escalated enforcement, performative compliance |
| Ejection Dynamics | Marginalisation of dissenters | Suppression of help-seeking, rights erosion |
| Compliance-Elasticity | CEG ≈ 0 or positive (substantive compliance maintained) | CEG \< 0 (inverse governance) |

## 6. Design Implications and Recommendations

Restoring authority-process alignment means pairing algorithmic rules
with human discretion in both DEI and governance contexts, and reducing
mediation lag with predictive models that anticipate pressure before it
breaches threshold. Success metrics need to be reframed around deep
integration, agency, and inclusion in decision-making rather than
surface counts, and low-cost dissent pathways need to exist so that
feedback and criticism don't themselves trigger performative compliance.

Programs should be linked to core objectives — embedded within primary
performance KPIs, so that internal belief shift accompanies external
image rather than substituting for it — and substantive outcomes should
be monitored quietly, through shadow metrics like cost per resolved
case, time to closure, and appeal reversal rates, compared against
performative compliance. Institutional safeguards should include
non-punitive measurement of substantive outcomes, reversibility through
sunset clauses and status-restoration pathways, a firm separation of
care from sanction so that help-seeking never increases liability, and
protection for hypothetical reasoning so that exploration is never
mistaken for confession.

## 7. Key Terms

A bounded system is one constrained by finite resources, norms, and
reputational thresholds. A second-order rule is an emergent constraint
that preserves surface stability rather than deep truth. Ejection
dynamics describe the systemic expulsion or marginalization of elements
threatening equilibrium. Image preservation is the prioritization of
public reputation over substantive change. Hollow authority is authority
claimed but lacking effective deterrence or mediation. The
compliance-elasticity of governance (CEG) is the ratio of substantive
compliance change to governance pressure change. Inverse governance is
the phase where increased governance pressure reduces compliance.

## Concluding Analysis

The analyses of DEI/affirmative action and governance inversion converge
on a single structural observation: authority without effective
mediation forces institutions to preserve image, generate second-order
rules, and eject disruptive variation. This self-reinforcing cycle
erodes substantive outcomes, produces backlash, and endangers
institutional stability. By recognizing the bounded-system dynamics,
monitoring compliance-elasticity, and redesigning incentives to align
authority with effective mediation, organizations and governments can
move from hollow authority and performative compliance to genuine,
transformative change.

## References

The literature this paper draws on and departs from includes research on
bias and DEI trainings, where many interventions fail or backfire and
effective designs require evidence-based approaches; studies on DEI
outcomes, which show initiatives producing both intended benefits and
unintended negative spillovers depending on implementation; resistance
literature linking pushback to complex individual and contextual factors
rather than simple prejudice; affirmative-action economic models
debating long-term equilibrium effects and merit perceptions; public
attitudes research showing mixed views, with some groups perceiving
harms in specific contexts; and control-theoretic, thermodynamic, and
categorical frameworks on compliance-elasticity and governance inversion
more broadly.

# Conclusion

“Protecting the Brand by Sacrificing the People”

This combined paper applies bounded-system theory and control-theoretic
analysis to explain how institutions invert under legitimacy pressure,
transforming governance from functional coordination into image
preservation. The contribution of Image Preservation, Ejection, and
Inverse Governance is therefore a structured account of its governing
constraints, mechanisms, and failure conditions. Its claims remain
bounded by the evidence and counterexamples specified in the paper, and
its practical value depends on whether the proposed relationships
improve explanation, prediction, or correction in the relevant domain.
The next paper (III.03) follows the same collapse dynamic into a
specific institutional setting: what happens to liability and oversight
once responsibility becomes too diffuse for anyone to hold.
