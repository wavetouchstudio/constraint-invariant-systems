# Abstract

“Protecting the Brand by Sacrificing the People”

The two bodies of work presented here converge on a single structural
principle: when institutions are bounded by finite resources, normative
mandates, and reputational thresholds, they respond to internal pressure
by preserving external image rather than integrating disruptive
variation. In diversity, equity, and inclusion (DEI) and
affirmative‑action (AA) programs, this manifests as second‑order rules
that prioritize metrics and surface compliance, triggering “ejection” of
dissenting actors. In governance systems more broadly, the same dynamics
appear as compliance‑elasticity inversion, whereby increased enforcement
intensity paradoxically reduces substantive compliance and heightens
harm. Together, these analyses illuminate how authority–process
decoupling and image preservation generate self‑reinforcing cycles that
erode substantive outcomes, produce backlash, and ultimately risk
institutional collapse. The paper offers a unified
framework—bounded‑system dynamics, second‑order rules, and
compliance‑elasticity—and proposes design interventions that restore
alignment between authority, mediation, and substantive transformation.

# 1. INTRODUCTION

## 1.1 Scope

Examination of DEI/AA programs as bounded systems that exhibit ejection
dynamics.

Analysis of governance inversion through compliance‑elasticity (CEG).

Demonstration that both domains share identical structural asymmetries.

## 1.2 Shared Foundations

Authority‑process decoupling: the right to act (authority) is separated
from the capacity to act effectively (process).

Image preservation: institutions maintain reputation by adhering to
surface norms.

Ejection dynamics: expulsion of elements that threaten equilibrium.

# 2. THEORETICAL BACKGROUND

## 2.1 Bounded‑System Dynamics

Finite resources, norms, and reputational thresholds limit tolerance for
deviation.

Small deviations tolerated; larger deviations trigger second‑order rules
that preserve image.

## 2.2 Second‑Order Rules & Image Preservation

\| Element \| Purpose \| Outcome \| Example \|

\|---------\|---------\|---------\|---------\|

\| Compliance & reporting \| Metric emphasis \| Appearing orderly \| DEI
dashboards \|

\| Broad commitment statements \| Stakeholder satisfaction \| Structural
inertia \| “We are committed to equity” \|

\| Procedural fixes \| Redirect criticism \| Limited substantive change
\| Token training modules \|

## 2.3 Authority‑Process Decoupling

Authority granted (e.g., right to enforce self‑defence, mandate DEI)
without effective mediation (e.g., lack of enforcement capacity, absence
of systemic change).

Result: hollow authority.

## 2.4 Compliance‑Elasticity of Governance (CEG)

CEG = ΔCₛ / ΔP_g

Cₛ: substantive compliance (alignment with safety, welfare, fairness).

P_g: governance pressure (enforcement intensity, rule density,
monitoring scope, penalty severity, narrative emphasis).

Phase interpretation

CEG \> 0: functional governance (negative feedback).

CEG ≈ 0: saturation.

CEG \< 0: inverse governance (positive feedback).

# 3. DEI/AA AS A BOUNDED‑SYSTEM MECHANISM

## 3.1 Functional Description

DEI: create inclusive environments reflecting demographic diversity.

AA: targeted measures (admissions, hiring) to offset historic
disadvantage.

## 3.2 Supporters’ Claims

# 1. Correct systemic discrimination.

# 2. Enhance fairness.

# 3. Improve performance through diverse perspectives.

## 3.3 Critics’ Concerns

# 1. Undermine merit perception.

# 2. Backfire, negative attitudes among non‑target groups.

## 3.4 Internal Pressures

\| Pressure \| Source \| Effect \|

\|----------\|--------\|--------\|

\| Normative \| Visibility of inequity \| Demand for corrective action
\|

\| Operational \| Implementation costs, resistance, conflicting metrics
\| Push for rapid, low‑cost solutions \|

## 3.5 Second‑Order Rules in DEI

Metric‑driven compliance.

Broad statements of commitment.

Procedural fixes rather than structural change.

## 3.6 Consequences

Emphasis on appearance, not internal adaptation.

Avoidance of hierarchy‑threatening change.

Marginalization of innovators if integration costs are high.

## 3.7 Ejection Dynamics

Marginalization or removal of DEI leaders.

Restructuring of DEI officers.

Scaling back initiatives.

Rhetoric shifts from integration to preservation.

## 3.8 Unintended Consequences (Ejection)

Token hires lacking full integration.

Talent diverted from core decision‑making.

Suppression of substantive debate.

## 3.9 Design Implications

Reframe success metrics toward deep integration and agency.

Create low‑cost dissent pathways.

Link DEI to core performance objectives.

Encourage iterative learning.

# 4. INVERSE GOVERNANCE AND COMPLIANCE‑ELASTICITY

## 4.1 Time Lags & Hysteresis

Short term: temporary compliance boost.

Medium term: plateau as adaptation spreads.

Long term: decline as evasion, burnout, legitimacy loss dominate.

## 4.2 Persistent Negative CEG

CEG \< 0 across multiple windows.

Accompanied by: rising evasion sophistication, increasing burnout,
performative compliance growth.

## 4.3 Human Consequences

\| Consequence \| Description \|

\|-------------\|-------------\|

\| Systemic burnout \| Workers and citizens exhausted by compliance
tasks \|

\| Performative compliance \| Surface safety, no real protection \|

\| Punishment of help‑seeking \| Risk signalling and exclusion \|

\| Rights erosion \| Abstraction and categorisation \|

\| Disproportionate harm \| Vulnerable populations disproportionately
affected \|

## 4.4 Three‑Condition Convergence

# 1. Evasion capacity \> enforcement capacity.

# 2. Legitimacy below threshold.

# 3. Coordination feasible.

If all hold, negative elasticity emerges.

## 4.5 Recursive Attribution Error

Visible compliance rises; substantive compliance falls.

Escalation trains evasion, expands documentation, narrows narratives,
converts ambiguity into suspicion.

## 4.6 Due Process Failure by Abstraction

Help‑seeking becomes risk signalling.

Care records become exclusionary flags.

Hypothetical reasoning misread as confession.

Subjective experience reclassified as pathology.

## 4.6 Institutional Blindness

Measurement misalignment.

Incentive capture (escalation rewarded).

Attribution error (blame individuals).

Narrative lock‑in (falling compliance = justification for more control).

## 4.7 Thermodynamic Constraint

Inefficiency grows linearly; inversion grows exponentially.

Requires energy beyond institutional capacity → burnout, exit cascades,
collapse.

# 6. DESIGN IMPLICATIONS & RECOMMENDATIONS

## 6.1 Restore Authority–Process Alignment

Hybrid mediation: algorithmic rules complemented by human discretion
(DEI & governance).

Reduce mediation lag with predictive models that anticipate pressure
before threshold breach.

## 6.2 Re‑frame Success Metrics

Deep integration, agency, and decision‑making inclusion rather than
surface counts.

## 6.3 Create Low‑Cost Dissent Pathways

Safe channels for feedback and criticism that do not trigger
performative compliance.

## 6.4 Link Programs to Core Objectives

Embed DEI and other reforms within primary performance KPIs.

Ensure internal belief shift accompanies external image.

## 6.5 Monitor Substantive Outcomes Quietly

Shadow metrics (cost per resolved case, time to closure, appeal reversal
rates).

Compare substantive versus performative compliance.

## 6.6 Institutional Safeguards

Non‑punitive measurement of substantive outcomes.

Reversibility: sunset clauses, status‑restoration pathways.

Separation of care and sanction: help‑seeking never increases liability.

Protected hypothetical reasoning: exploration ≠ confession.

## Concluding Analysis

The analyses of DEI/affirmative action and governance inversion converge
on a single structural observation: authority without effective
mediation forces institutions to preserve image, generate second‑order
rules, and eject disruptive variation. This self‑reinforcing cycle
erodes substantive outcomes, produces backlash, and endangers
institutional stability. By recognising the bounded‑system dynamics,
monitoring compliance‑elasticity, and redesigning incentives to align
authority with effective mediation, organisations and governments can
move from hollow authority and performative compliance to genuine,
transformative change.

# Conclusion

This combined paper applies bounded-system theory and control-theoretic
analysis to explain how institutions invert under legitimacy pressure,
transforming governance from functional coordination into image
preservation. The contribution of Image Preservation, Ejection, and
Inverse Governance is therefore a structured account of its governing
constraints, mechanisms, and failure conditions. Its claims remain
bounded by the evidence and counterexamples specified in the paper, and
its practical value depends on whether the proposed relationships
improve explanation, prediction, or correction in the relevant domain.
