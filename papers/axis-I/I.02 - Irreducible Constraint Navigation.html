Abstract

“Thinking Through Walls”

This paper turns the structural lens of Constraint-Invariant Systems Research inward, from institutional failure to the experience of thinking itself. It argues that core phenomenological features of conscious experience—felt effort, path-dependence, and the emergence of global coherence only after a decision is closed—can be modeled using irreducibly constrained optimization problems such as the Traveling Salesman Problem. In such problems, no local shortcut preserves global optimality, forcing the system to confront genuine constraint rather than heuristic approximation. By defining resistance as the Shannon entropy of remaining compatible states, the framework shows that subjective cognitive load naturally exhibits nonlinear, spike-like, and incompressible dynamics analogous to computational hardness. These dynamics align with reported experiences of mental effort, indecision, and sudden clarity following commitment. Crucially, consciousness is proposed to arise when a self-modeling system not only encounters resistance but internalizes and reacts to it, transforming abstract constraint pressure into felt effort. Consciousness is thus reframed as the inside perspective of navigating an irreducible constraint space rather than as a mysterious emergent property. The model unifies computational complexity, information theory, and phenomenology, yielding concrete, falsifiable predictions regarding decision latency, heuristic substitution, and the heightened difficulty of globally coupled choices.

Why Conscious Thought Feels Effortful

I. The Problem of Effort

Some decisions collapse quickly: list the pros and cons, rehearse the arguments, simulate outcomes, and a choice lands. Other decisions refuse to settle. You can weigh evidence, run mental simulations, exhaust your data—and still feel resistance that isn't confusion or lack of information but something more fundamental.

This paper proposes a structural answer: conscious deliberation resembles navigation through an irreducibly coupled constraint space—one in which global coherence cannot be evaluated locally and resistance is an unavoidable informational cost rather than a sign of poor optimization.

II. When Local Decisions Aren't Enough

In many computational problems, local choices suffice. Dynamic programming succeeds because optimal substructure exists: solve small pieces correctly and the global solution follows. But some problems resist this decomposition.

The canonical example is the Traveling Salesman Problem (TSP): given cities and distances, find the shortest tour that visits each exactly once. The difficulty isn't scale alone—it's coupling. Choosing one edge changes the compatibility of all future edges; an efficient early move can force catastrophic late detours; local optimality does not imply global optimality.

This is what makes TSP irreducible: there is no reliable shortcut, and evaluation is delayed until closure.

III. Irreducibility as Structure

Irreducible constraint spaces share three properties. First, global coupling: every choice reshapes the remaining space. Second, delayed evaluation: true cost is revealed only after closure. Third, path dependence: early commitments cannot be undone without global cost.

When such a system is navigated under limited computational resources, uncertainty accumulates as entropy over remaining compatible states—that entropy is what we will call resistance.

IV. Resistance as Informational Cost

After decisions, let Ω_k be the remaining feasible completions. Define resistance R_k = H(Ω_k), the Shannon entropy of those states. Resistance is not energy or distance; it is irreducible uncertainty about whether the current trajectory can reconcile all constraints.

Resistance behaves nonlinearly: it spikes when an early commitment collapses many futures, plateaus when multiple compatible completions remain, and vanishes only at closure. In irreducible systems, resistance cannot be eliminated by local heuristics alone—it is structural.

V. The Structural Isomorphism

Now consider conscious deliberation. We observe effort and fatigue, decisions that reshape identity, delayed coherence, irreversible commitments, and retrospective meaning. These properties map onto irreducible optimization: a partial tour corresponds to a partial self-model, an edge choice to a commitment or reaction, global tour cost to narrative coherence, entropy over completions to felt effort, and closure to meaning.

In both cases, local evaluation is insufficient; early steps constrain future compatibility; resistance is the only continuous signal. The claim is not that the brain solves NP-complete problems—the claim is structural: conscious deliberation has the same constraint geometry.

VI. Why It Feels Like Something

Many physical systems exhibit resistance, but that alone isn't consciousness. The difference is self-modeling: a system that tracks not only its transitions but its difficulty navigating them.

When resistance becomes internally accessible, it modulates behavior, guides search, and is experienced as subjective effort—corresponding to internally represented irreducible reconciliation cost.

VII. Identity as Path Dependence

Identity formation mirrors constraint navigation: beliefs adopted for local stability may later restrict coherence, and coping strategies that minimize short-term resistance can increase long-term cost.

Just as in TSP, early efficient edges can force costly closure. The self is not merely maximizing reward—it is navigating a path-dependent constraint landscape under uncertainty.

VIII. Predictions

This framework generates testable predictions: first, effort correlates with entropy rather than option count—two decisions with equal options but different constraint coupling should produce different subjective difficulty. Second, deliberation time scales nonlinearly with global constraint coupling. Third, heuristics mirror approximation algorithms: greedy intuition corresponds to nearest-neighbor search, reconsideration to 2-opt local search, and simulation to parallel candidate pruning.

IX. Position Among Theories

This model integrates with several existing frameworks: predictive processing, where resistance resembles irreducible prediction error; the Free Energy Principle, where irreducible constraint pressure limits compression; and integrated information theory, where resistance scales with irreducible integration.

Predictive Processing, the Free Energy Principle, and Integrated Information Theory are each contested within philosophy of mind and cognitive science, with active rival accounts—higher-order theories, global workspace theory—that this paper does not engage. Citing compatibility with one contested theory is not independent support; the claim here should be read as one candidate functional account among several, not as a synthesis validated by its resemblance to existing frameworks.

Not representation alone. Not complexity alone. But irreducible reconciliation cost under self-modeling.

X. Reframing the Hard Problem

The traditional question asks why computation produces experience. This framework reframes it: what kind of computation necessarily produces experienced resistance? The answer proposed here is navigation of irreducibly coupled constraint spaces by systems that model their own difficulty under limited resources.

One-Sentence Summary

Conscious experience arises when a self-modeling system navigates an irreducibly coupled constraint space under resource limits, internally tracking the entropy of remaining compatible states as felt resistance. Conclusion

“Thinking Through Walls”

This paper argues that core phenomenological features of conscious experience—felt effort, path-dependence, and the emergence of global coherence only after a decision is closed—can be modeled using irreducibly constrained optimization problems such as the Traveling Salesman Problem. The contribution of Irreducible Constraint Navigation is therefore a structured account of its governing constraints, mechanisms, and failure conditions.
