VII.09 - Latent-Variable Consciousness: Pluralistic Meaning Coordination

A state-space framework for empirical comparison across competing
theories of consciousness

# Abstract

“Consciousness as the Missing Variable”

Where VII.08 examined self-referential alignment at the level of
compositional metaphysics, this paper narrows that same question to a
concrete methodological proposal for consciousness research. It develops
a theory-agnostic method for studying consciousness as a latent
variable: an unobserved state inferred from converging physiological,
neural, behavioral, and first-person indicators. The proposal does not
treat statistical usefulness as proof of a metaphysical entity. Instead,
it asks whether a shared latent state improves prediction, survives
intervention, and transports across measurement systems better than
models that omit it. The framework separates latent dynamics from
system-specific observation functions, identifies the conditions
required to avoid circularity, and shows how Global Workspace Theory,
Integrated Information Theory, predictive processing, and related
accounts can be represented as competing constraints rather than
identity commitments. A second application concerns pluralistic meaning
coordination. When beliefs are tied to identity, theory disputes become
socially adversarial even when scientific selection can proceed through
comparative model performance. Latent-variable modeling offers a shared
interface through which incompatible interpretations can generate
commensurable predictions without requiring a single worldview.

## 1. Problem and Scope

Consciousness is directly available only from a first-person
perspective, while scientific inquiry depends on publicly comparable
observations. Neural activity, verbal report, task performance,
autonomic response, and behavioral flexibility are measurable, but none
is identical to the phenomenon being explained. The methodological
problem is therefore one of inference: how can a scientific model
represent an unobserved experiential or functional state without
defining that state by the very observations it is later used to
predict?

The proposed answer is to model consciousness as a latent state C(t).
C(t) is not observed directly. It is estimated from multiple indicator
families and assigned its own temporal dynamics. Each measurement system
receives a separate observation function, allowing indicators to vary
without assuming that the underlying state must vary in exactly the same
way.

This is a methodological proposal, not a settled theory of
consciousness. A successful latent model would establish predictive and
explanatory utility within a defined domain; it would not, by itself,
resolve whether consciousness is fundamental, emergent, computational,
biological, or metaphysical.

## 2. Core Definitions

Latent state. An unobserved variable inferred from patterned relations
among observed variables.

Indicator. A measurable quantity—such as report accuracy, neural
integration, autonomic response, or behavioral adaptation—that may carry
information about the latent state.

State dynamics. A rule describing how C(t) changes over time in response
to its prior state, inputs, interventions, and noise.

Observation function. A system-specific mapping from C(t) to an expected
measurement.

Measurement invariance. The degree to which an indicator retains a
stable relation to the latent construct across persons, contexts,
instruments, or systems.

Structural transportability. Preservation of the latent dynamics when
the model is applied to a new measurement system.

Identification. The use of anchors, constraints, priors, or
interventions sufficient to distinguish the model’s parameters and
scale.

## 3. State-Space Formulation

A minimal formulation separates the evolution of the latent state from
the production of observable measurements. In discrete time, the
structure can be written as:

Cₜ = f(Cₜ₋₁, Uₜ, εₜ)

Yₖ,ₜ = gₖ(Cₜ, Xₖ,ₜ, ηₖ,ₜ)

Here, Cₜ is the latent state; Uₜ represents inputs or interventions; εₜ
represents process noise; Yₖ,ₜ is the observation produced by
measurement system k; Xₖ,ₜ contains system-specific covariates; gₖ is
that system’s observation function; and ηₖ,ₜ is measurement noise.
Continuous-time ordinary or stochastic differential equations can
replace the first equation when temporal resolution and domain knowledge
warrant them. Hidden Markov models are appropriate when the proposed
states are categorical or regime-like.

### 3.1 Indicator families

A defensible model should draw from indicators that fail in different
ways. Neural measures may be sensitive to instrumentation and
preprocessing; reports may depend on memory, language, and demand
characteristics; behavior may reflect competence without experience;
physiological measures may track arousal rather than consciousness.
Agreement among partially independent channels is more informative than
repeated measurements from a single channel.

| Indicator family | Possible measures | Primary confounds |
|----|----|----|
| First-person report | confidence, vividness, continuity, qualitative description | language, memory, compliance |
| Behavior | discrimination, flexible transfer, error correction | training, motor capacity, automatic processing |
| Neural activity | broadcast, integration, complexity, connectivity | analysis choices, arousal, task demands |
| Physiology | pupil response, autonomic change, sleep or sedation markers | stress, medication, general activation |

### 3.2 Interventions and conditions

Meditation, ritual, psychoactive substances, sedation, sleep
transitions, injury, sensory deprivation, and task load can be
represented as inputs or interventions rather than as definitions of the
latent state. Their value lies in producing patterned changes that
distinguish rival models. Ethical and legal constraints govern which
interventions are appropriate; observational variation can be used when
direct manipulation is unsafe or impossible.

## 4. Avoiding Circularity

Circularity occurs when consciousness is defined by a single outcome and
then invoked to explain that same outcome. The latent construction
avoids this only if its inferential design imposes independent tests.
Four requirements are central:

Multiple indicators. Estimate C(t) from several indicator families
rather than from the target outcome alone.

Identification. Fix the latent scale through anchors, priors, reference
conditions, or invariance constraints.

Out-of-sample prediction. Evaluate whether the model predicts withheld
observations better than models without C(t).

Interventional validation. Test whether manipulating an input changes
C(t) and downstream indicators in the direction and temporal order
predicted.

The strongest test is comparative. A latent model should be evaluated
against simpler observable-only models, separate latent variables for
each indicator family, and alternative causal structures. If the shared
latent adds complexity without improving prediction, calibration,
intervention response, or compression, it has not earned inclusion.

## 5. Cross-System Transportability

The central transportability hypothesis is that a genuine shared latent
structure should not need to be reinvented for every measurement system.
A model estimated from one domain should retain at least part of its
state-transition structure when applied to another domain. The new
domain may require a new observation function gₖ, but it should not
require arbitrary replacement of f, the dynamics of C(t).

Transportability does not mean identical numerical scores. The origin
and scale of a latent variable are generally arbitrary unless anchored.
The relevant tests concern structural invariance, temporal ordering,
cross-modal prediction, and response to intervention. Failure to
transport is informative: it may indicate that the latent variable is
too broad, that the indicators measure different constructs, or that the
dynamics genuinely differ across systems.

## 6. Relation to Existing Theories

Global Workspace Theory, Integrated Information Theory,
recurrent-processing accounts, higher-order theories, and
predictive-processing approaches need not be collapsed into one
doctrine. Each can constrain a different part of the model: the
indicator set, the transition dynamics, the observation functions, or
the intervention predictions. Their commitments can then be compared
through explicit models rather than treated primarily as rival
identities.

| Theory family | Possible model constraint | Discriminating question |
|----|----|----|
| Global workspace | broadcast or global availability indicators | Does inferred C(t) predict flexible cross-module access? |
| Integrated information | integration or differentiation measures | Do proposed integration measures improve cross-condition prediction? |
| Predictive processing | hierarchical prediction-error dynamics | Do precision changes explain transitions and intervention effects? |
| Higher-order accounts | metarepresentational indicators | Is higher-order representation necessary for the relevant state changes? |

This comparison remains neutral only if failure is possible. A
theory-mapped model must specify what would count against it, and model
selection criteria such as cross-validation, leave-one-out prediction,
information criteria, calibration, and intervention performance must be
chosen before inspecting the favored result.

## 7. Meaning Coordination as a Second-Level Application

Disputes about consciousness operate simultaneously at scientific and
social levels. Scientifically, models compete through explanatory reach,
predictive accuracy, and reproducible intervention results. Socially,
accounts of consciousness often support identities, moral commitments,
religious meanings, or views of human uniqueness. Criticism of a model
can therefore be experienced as criticism of the person or community
that holds it.

Decentralized knowledge production intensifies this tension. No single
authority can reliably canonize meaning for all participants, while
digital communication increases the speed, volume, and visibility of
incompatible claims. The result may include attention fragmentation,
polarization, and symbolic conflict. Describing this as a reorganization
rather than an inevitable collapse is a hypothesis about system
dynamics, not a guarantee of benign outcomes.

### 7.1 Distinguishing levels

| Level | Selection pressure | Typical failure |
|----|----|----|
| Scientific | prediction, intervention, replication, comparative fit | protecting a theory from disconfirmation |
| Social | identity, belonging, legitimacy, moral meaning | treating disagreement as existential attack |
| Meta-coordination | continued communication across incompatible commitments | mistaking coexistence for factual equivalence |

### 7.2 Pluralism without relativism

Pluralistic coordination does not require all claims to be equally true.
It requires a shared procedure for translating claims into definitions,
indicators, predictions, and acknowledged limits. Participants may
retain distinct metaphysical interpretations while cooperating on
experiments and accepting common standards for empirical claims.
Identity stability can support this process when it reduces the need to
defend every belief as a condition of personal continuity.

Appreciation and similarity-finding can reduce identity threat, but they
are coordination techniques, not substitutes for criticism. Productive
pluralism combines interpretive charity with explicit tests, clear
boundaries, and protection for dissent. The desired settlement is
therefore a meta-norm: multiple systems of meaning may coexist while
factual disagreements remain open to disciplined comparison.

## 8. Research Program and Falsifiable Expectations

The framework produces a staged research program:

Specify the construct and domain. State which aspect of consciousness
C(t) is intended to represent and which aspects it excludes.

Select partially independent indicators. Document their confounds and
expected temporal relationships.

Fit rival models. Compare a shared latent state with observable-only,
multiple-latent, and theory-specific alternatives.

Test transportability. Hold the latent dynamics fixed where possible and
estimate new observation functions in a second domain.

Apply ethical interventions. Evaluate predicted changes, recovery
trajectories, and cross-modal effects.

Report failures. Treat non-invariance, weak identification, and absent
predictive gain as evidence requiring revision or rejection.

Evidence against the proposal would include persistent
non-identifiability, no advantage over simpler models, dependence on a
single indicator family, failure under intervention, or the need to
redefine the latent dynamics for every new system. Evidence in its favor
would be graded rather than absolute: replicable predictive gain, stable
temporal structure, cross-system transport, and successful novel
intervention predictions.

## 9. Implications

### 9.1 Scientific practice

The model encourages researchers to separate observations from
ontological conclusions and to make theory comparison explicit. It also
supports cumulative work: new instruments can be introduced through new
observation functions without discarding the entire model.

### 9.2 Institutional design

Institutions coordinating contentious research can distinguish empirical
standards from worldview membership. Shared repositories, preregistered
comparisons, adversarial collaboration, and transparent model criticism
can reduce the incentive to convert methodological disagreement into
identity conflict.

### 9.3 Individual interpretation

For individuals, the framework offers a disciplined distinction between
experience, measurement, model, and metaphysical interpretation. This
distinction permits serious engagement with subjective reports without
treating every interpretation of those reports as equally supported.

## 10. Limitations

Latent variables can create an illusion of explanation when they merely
rename covariance. Results may depend heavily on indicator selection,
prior assumptions, measurement quality, and population boundaries.
First-person reports remain difficult to compare, and no statistical
architecture eliminates the ethical or philosophical questions raised by
altered states, impaired communication, nonhuman animals, or artificial
systems. The meaning-coordination extension is likewise a conceptual
proposal; it requires evidence before claims about polarization
reduction or institutional performance can be generalized.

# 11. Conclusion

“Consciousness as the Missing Variable”

Consciousness can be modeled as a latent state without being declared a
hidden substance or reduced to a single proxy. The framework separates
latent dynamics from observation, demands independent indicators,
requires out-of-sample and interventional tests, and treats cross-system
transportability as a central criterion. Existing theories become
explicit sources of competing model constraints rather than identities
that must defeat one another wholesale. Applied to pluralistic meaning
coordination, the same structure allows communities to preserve
interpretive differences while sharing procedures for empirical
comparison. The proposal succeeds only where it produces identifiable
models, risky predictions, and transportable results; otherwise, the
latent variable should be revised or removed.
