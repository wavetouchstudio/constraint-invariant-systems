HELD

Explicit epistemic-boundary section, careful hypothesis framing.

Abstract

“Nobody Decided This, But Everyone Did It”

The previous paper (VII.04) modeled how language itself has to switch registers under asymmetric pressure; this paper steps back from wording to the harder question underneath it-what asymmetry and constraint actually excuse, and what they don't. It develops a structural distinction between constraints and agency: constraints delimit possible action, while agents select and perform actions within those limits. Treating constraints as if they choose outcomes produces fatalism and responsibility vacuums, while treating agency as unconstrained obscures physical, informational, institutional, and interpersonal limits, so the framework localizes authorship without denying causal context. Alignment is defined provisionally as increasing coherence among intention, action, observable consequence, and invariant constraints-not obedience to an authority or doctrine-and cooperation may emerge when agents can recognize compatible action and maintain correction pathways, though neither universality nor harmony is guaranteed. The result is a bounded model of responsibility that preserves dignity, accountability, and plural coordination while rejecting both metaphysical determinism and context-free individualism.

1. The Structural Problem

Explanatory systems often migrate from describing conditions to assigning authorship. History, biology, economics, institutions, algorithms, fate, or divinity may be treated as if they acted in place of the people and organizations operating through them, and this shift can illuminate constraint while simultaneously dissolving responsibility.

The corrective is not to deny structure-it is to keep different explanatory roles separate: constraints shape the available paths, causes influence which paths become likely, and agents exercise whatever selection, authorization, maintenance, or refusal capacity they actually possess.

2. Core Terms

Constraint. A logical, physical, structural, informational, institutional, or situational condition that limits or shapes the available action space.

Agency. A bounded capacity to select, authorize, initiate, maintain, revise, or refuse action. The degree of agency is an empirical question, not an all-or-nothing moral label.

Local authorship. Responsibility assigned to the actors and decision points that possess relevant control, knowledge, or correction capacity in a specific event.

Precursor constraint structure. The paper's proposed name for the enabling topology within which realized action occurs-a conceptual model, not an empirically demonstrated metaphysical entity.

Alignment. A proposed relation of increasing coherence among intention, action, consequence, and applicable constraints; it does not establish moral correctness or institutional legitimacy by itself.

3. Constraint and Agency Are Not Opposites

Table 1. Constraint and agency as distinct explanatory roles

Dimension Constraint Agency
Function Delimits, enables, or changes the cost of possibilities Selects, authorizes, maintains, revises, or refuses within bounded possibilities
Temporal role May precede or persist across an event Operates through situated decision points
Normative status Does not become good or legitimate merely by existing Does not become unconstrained merely because choice is present
Failure of attribution Constraint described as an actor Context ignored and responsibility overstated

The original formulation-constraints enable action, agents perform it-functions best as an attribution rule rather than a complete theory of causation. Constraints can still exert causal influence by changing feasibility, incentives, information, timing, or risk; they simply do not possess intention just because they affect outcomes. That constraining role shows up in several recognizable forms: logical consistency and mathematical relations delimit which descriptions can be coherent, physical law and material conditions delimit what action is feasible, information and uncertainty delimit what an actor can know, institutions and interfaces and incentives shape cost and opportunity, and coercion, disability, dependency, and unequal exposure can sharply reduce practical choice.

4. The Precursor Model

The precursor constraint structure names the total set of conditions that must be satisfied for a realized world and its actions to be coherent. In this framework it precedes action logically, not necessarily temporally, and does not issue commands, intervene, or compete with situated agents.

Epistemic boundary. Because this is a metaphysical and systems-theoretic proposal, it should not be presented as settled fact. Its practical value lies in testing whether explanations can acknowledge universal limits without assigning intention to those limits or exporting responsibility beyond the actors who can actually act.

5. Agency Is Local and Graded

Agency is local because action occurs through identifiable sites of control-individuals, teams, institutions, interfaces, or delegated systems-though locality does not mean that only isolated individuals matter, since organizations and automated systems can distribute agency across design, authorization, deployment, oversight, maintenance, and correction roles.

Agency is also graded: capacity varies with knowledge, coercion, alternatives, developmental status, material dependence, and the ability to predict or correct consequences. A useful attribution therefore asks who could select, stop, revise, disclose, or repair, rather than merely who was nearest to the visible outcome, which the following sequence lays out as a working procedure.

  1. Identify the outcome and window. Pin down the relevant outcome and the action window in which it took shape.

  2. Map the constraints. Trace the material, informational, legal, and social constraints in play.

  3. Locate the decision points. Find the decision, authorization, maintenance, and correction points that actually existed.

  4. Estimate exposure. Estimate each actor's knowledge, alternatives, and exposure to coercion.

  5. Assign proportionally. Assign responsibility proportionally and preserve routes for revision.

6. Reclaiming Agency Without Blaming the Constrained

Returning authorship to local actors can restore dignity and correction capacity, but it becomes harmful if used to erase structural coercion or blame people for conditions they could not control. The framework must therefore hold two facts together: context constrains choice, and remaining choice can still matter.

7. Alignment Along Constraints

Alignment in this paper is not conformity to doctrine. It is a proposed diagnostic relation in which intentions, methods, and consequences become less mutually contradictory while respecting relevant constraints, and the concept remains incomplete without specifying whose goals, which consequences, what time horizon, and what evidence counts as coherence. In practice that means respecting physical and logical limits rather than substituting narrative for feasibility, comparing stated intentions with observable methods and consequences, preserving feedback that can reveal error, revising action when consequences contradict the model, and avoiding any claim that one culture, institution, or tradition monopolizes access to constraint.

8. Structural Mobility

The source calls increased capacity for abstraction, responsibility, and coordination "upper mobility within the set." To avoid confusion with social hierarchy, this paper uses structural mobility instead: the ability to operate across more complex constraints without losing traceability, correction, or local authorship.

Structural mobility is a hypothesis, not a guaranteed reward for alignment. Increased coherence may reduce some forms of friction, but compatible goals, resources, trust, and institutional support still matter.

9. Cooperation Without Coercion

Cooperation can emerge when agents' actions are mutually intelligible, correction costs remain tolerable, and compatible trajectories reduce interference, since shared constraints can support coordination without requiring identical metaphysics or collective identity.

The stronger original claim-that aligned agents naturally cooperate-requires qualification, because coherence within one agent does not guarantee compatible goals between agents. Scarcity, asymmetric power, incompatible preferences, or strategic deception may still require governance, negotiation, safeguards, or separation, which is why cooperation is better treated as a practice than a guarantee.

  1. Make constraints visible. Make constraints and decision rights visible to everyone involved.

  2. Exchange outcome-based feedback. Trade feedback on outcomes rather than enforcing identity agreement.

  3. Preserve exit. Preserve voluntary exit and non-punitive correction wherever feasible.

  4. Coordinate on interfaces. Coordinate around compatible actions and measurable interfaces rather than shared belief.

  5. Reassess under asymmetry. Reassess the arrangement when costs or consequences become asymmetrical.

10. Narrative Reconciliation

Cultural, religious, and political narratives can be treated as symbolic compressions of attempts to navigate constraint. This framing may help translate across vocabularies, but it should not presume that all narratives encode the same structure or are equally compatible with evidence.

Reconciliation is therefore local and procedural: identify shared constraints, translate claims into consequences where possible, preserve meaningful difference, and decline forced synthesis when contradictions remain.

11. Failure Modes and Limits

Several recurring failure modes threaten this framework from opposite directions. Fatalism treats constraint as authorship, so action appears pointless, while voluntarism treats agency as unlimited, so structural harm disappears from view. Authority capture defines alignment as whatever whoever controls interpretation says it is, and metaphysical inflation asserts the precursor model as empirical fact rather than a working hypothesis. Coherence laundering mistakes internal consistency for truth or ethics, locality abuse pushes responsibility onto the most visible low-power actor, and the harmony assumption presumes that compatible constraint navigation eliminates conflict on its own.

This framework cannot by itself measure agency, settle disputed causation, identify moral truth, or guarantee cooperation. Its narrower contribution is to improve attribution by separating enabling conditions, causal influence, situated control, and responsibility.

12. Applications

Plural coordination Coordinate around interfaces and outcomes Do not force metaphysical or identity agreement

13. Proposed Evaluation Questions

  1. Constraint without intention. Does the explanation name constraints without attributing intention to them?

  2. Alternatives specified. Are meaningful alternatives, knowledge, coercion, and correction capacity specified?

  3. Proportional responsibility. Is responsibility proportional to actual control rather than visibility?

  4. Observable alignment. Are alignment claims tied to observable intention-action-consequence relations?

  5. Room to disagree. Can disagreement, exit, and revision occur without identity punishment?

One-Sentence Summary

Distinguishing constraints from agency lets a system localize authorship, define alignment as coherence rather than obedience, and treat cooperation as contingent rather than guaranteed, without either excusing structural harm as destiny or erasing it as mere choice; from here, the next paper (VII.06) turns to a related failure of attribution one level up-what happens when reference accumulates faster than understanding, and confidence gets mistaken for integration.

Conclusion

“Nobody Decided This, But Everyone Did It”

Constraints bound and shape action; agents exercise bounded capacities within those conditions. Keeping these functions distinct prevents metaphysical or institutional explanations from silently becoming substitutes for responsibility, and it also prevents responsibility language from erasing coercion and structural limitation.

Local agency is best understood as traceable, graded authorship. Alignment is a proposed coherence relation rather than obedience, and cooperation may emerge from compatible action under shared constraints, though it remains contingent on power, resources, feedback, and exit. Properly limited, the framework preserves dignity and correction capacity without denying either structure or conflict.

Key Contributions

  • An attribution rule separating constraint, causal influence, agency, and responsibility.

  • A graded account of local authorship that includes distributed organizational and technical roles.

  • A qualified definition of alignment centered on intention, action, consequence, and correction.

  • Safeguards against fatalism, context erasure, authority capture, metaphysical inflation, and responsibility dumping.

  • A practical sequence for mapping agency without treating structural constraint as destiny.
