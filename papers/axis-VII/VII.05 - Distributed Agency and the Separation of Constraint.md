VII.05 - Distributed Agency and the Separation of Constraint

Local authorship, bounded possibility, and cooperation without moral
outsourcing

# Abstract

“Nobody Decided This, But Everyone Did It”

This paper develops a structural distinction between constraints and
agency. Constraints delimit possible action; agents select and perform
actions within those limits. Treating constraints as if they choose
outcomes produces fatalism and responsibility vacuums, while treating
agency as unconstrained obscures physical, informational, institutional,
and interpersonal limits. The framework therefore localizes authorship
without denying causal context. Alignment is defined provisionally as
increasing coherence among intention, action, observable consequence,
and invariant constraints—not obedience to an authority or doctrine.
Cooperation may emerge when agents can recognize compatible action and
maintain correction pathways, but neither universality nor harmony is
guaranteed. The result is a bounded model of responsibility that
preserves dignity, accountability, and plural coordination while
rejecting both metaphysical determinism and context-free individualism.

# 1. The Structural Problem

Explanatory systems often migrate from describing conditions to
assigning authorship. History, biology, economics, institutions,
algorithms, fate, or divinity may be treated as if they acted in place
of the people and organizations operating through them. This shift can
illuminate constraint while simultaneously dissolving responsibility.

The corrective is not to deny structure. It is to keep different
explanatory roles separate: constraints shape the available paths;
causes influence which paths become likely; and agents exercise whatever
selection, authorization, maintenance, or refusal capacity they actually
possess.

# 2. Core Terms

**Constraint.** A logical, physical, structural, informational,
institutional, or situational condition that limits or shapes the
available action space.

**Agency.** A bounded capacity to select, authorize, initiate, maintain,
revise, or refuse action. The degree of agency is an empirical question,
not an all-or-nothing moral label.

**Local authorship.** Responsibility assigned to the actors and decision
points that possess relevant control, knowledge, or correction capacity
in a specific event.

**Precursor constraint structure.** The paper's proposed name for the
enabling topology within which realized action occurs. It is a
conceptual model, not an empirically demonstrated metaphysical entity.

**Alignment.** A proposed relation of increasing coherence among
intention, action, consequence, and applicable constraints. It does not
establish moral correctness or institutional legitimacy by itself.

# 3. Constraint and Agency Are Not Opposites

Table 1. Constraint and agency as distinct explanatory roles

| **Dimension** | **Constraint** | **Agency** |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Function | Delimits, enables, or changes the cost of possibilities | Selects, authorizes, maintains, revises, or refuses within bounded possibilities |
| Temporal role | May precede or persist across an event | Operates through situated decision points |
| Normative status | Does not become good or legitimate merely by existing | Does not become unconstrained merely because choice is present |
| Failure of attribution | Constraint described as an actor | Context ignored and responsibility overstated |

The original formulation—constraints enable action; agents perform
it—functions best as an attribution rule, not a complete theory of
causation. Constraints can exert causal influence by changing
feasibility, incentives, information, timing, or risk. They still do not
possess intention simply because they affect outcomes.

- Logical consistency and mathematical relations delimit coherent
  descriptions.

- Physical law and material conditions delimit feasible action.

- Information and uncertainty delimit what an actor can know.

- Institutions, interfaces, and incentives shape cost and opportunity.

- Coercion, disability, dependency, and unequal exposure can sharply
  reduce practical choice.

# 4. The Precursor Model

The precursor constraint structure names the total set of conditions
that must be satisfied for a realized world and its actions to be
coherent. In this framework it precedes action logically, not
necessarily temporally, and does not issue commands, intervene, or
compete with situated agents.

Because this is a metaphysical and systems-theoretic proposal, it should
not be presented as settled fact. Its practical value lies in testing
whether explanations can acknowledge universal limits without assigning
intention to those limits or exporting responsibility beyond the actors
who can act.

# 5. Agency Is Local and Graded

Agency is local because action occurs through identifiable sites of
control: individuals, teams, institutions, interfaces, or delegated
systems. Locality does not mean that only isolated individuals matter.
Organizations and automated systems can distribute agency across design,
authorization, deployment, oversight, maintenance, and correction roles.

Agency is also graded. Capacity varies with knowledge, coercion,
alternatives, developmental status, material dependence, and the ability
to predict or correct consequences. A useful attribution therefore asks
who could select, stop, revise, disclose, or repair—not merely who was
nearest to the visible outcome.

1.  Identify the relevant outcome and action window.

2.  Map material, informational, legal, and social constraints.

3.  Locate decision, authorization, maintenance, and correction points.

4.  Estimate each actor's knowledge, alternatives, and exposure to
    coercion.

5.  Assign responsibility proportionally and preserve routes for
    revision.

# 6. Reclaiming Agency Without Blaming the Constrained

Returning authorship to local actors can restore dignity and correction
capacity, but it becomes harmful if used to erase structural coercion or
blame people for conditions they could not control. The framework must
therefore hold two facts together: context constrains choice, and
remaining choice can still matter.

Table 2. Attribution failures and corrective questions

| **Failure mode** | **Distortion** | **Correction** |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Moral outsourcing | Systems or abstractions are treated as the sole author | Locate concrete control and correction capacity |
| Context erasure | All outcomes are reduced to unconstrained personal choice | Map coercion, dependency, information, and alternatives |
| Obedience substitution | Alignment becomes compliance with authority | Evaluate intention-action-consequence coherence |
| Responsibility diffusion | Distributed participation means no one is accountable | Trace design, authorization, maintenance, and oversight roles |

# 7. Alignment Along Constraints

Alignment in this paper is not conformity to doctrine. It is a proposed
diagnostic relation: intentions, methods, and consequences become less
mutually contradictory while respecting relevant constraints. The
concept remains incomplete without specifying whose goals, which
consequences, what time horizon, and what evidence counts as coherence.

- Respecting physical and logical limits rather than substituting
  narrative for feasibility.

- Comparing stated intentions with observable methods and consequences.

- Preserving feedback that can reveal error.

- Revising action when consequences contradict the model.

- Avoiding claims that one culture, institution, or tradition
  monopolizes access to constraint.

# 8. Structural Mobility

The source calls increased capacity for abstraction, responsibility, and
coordination 'upper mobility within the set.' To avoid confusion with
social hierarchy, this paper uses structural mobility: the ability to
operate across more complex constraints without losing traceability,
correction, or local authorship.

Structural mobility is a hypothesis, not a guaranteed reward for
alignment. Increased coherence may reduce some forms of friction, but
compatible goals, resources, trust, and institutional support still
matter.

# 9. Cooperation Without Coercion

Cooperation can emerge when agents' actions are mutually intelligible,
correction costs remain tolerable, and compatible trajectories reduce
interference. Shared constraints can support coordination without
requiring identical metaphysics or collective identity.

The stronger original claim—that aligned agents naturally
cooperate—requires qualification. Coherence within one agent does not
guarantee compatible goals between agents. Scarcity, asymmetric power,
incompatible preferences, or strategic deception may still require
governance, negotiation, safeguards, or separation.

1.  Make constraints and decision rights visible.

2.  Exchange outcome-based feedback rather than enforcing identity
    agreement.

3.  Preserve voluntary exit and non-punitive correction where feasible.

4.  Coordinate around compatible actions and measurable interfaces.

5.  Reassess when costs or consequences become asymmetrical.

# 10. Narrative Reconciliation

Cultural, religious, and political narratives can be treated as symbolic
compressions of attempts to navigate constraint. This framing may help
translate across vocabularies, but it should not presume that all
narratives encode the same structure or are equally compatible with
evidence.

Reconciliation is therefore local and procedural: identify shared
constraints, translate claims into consequences where possible, preserve
meaningful difference, and decline forced synthesis when contradictions
remain.

# 11. Failure Modes and Limits

- Fatalism: constraint is treated as authorship and action appears
  pointless.

- Voluntarism: agency is treated as unlimited and structural harm
  disappears.

- Authority capture: alignment is defined by whoever controls
  interpretation.

- Metaphysical inflation: the precursor model is asserted as empirical
  fact.

- Coherence laundering: internal consistency is mistaken for truth or
  ethics.

- Locality abuse: responsibility is pushed onto the most visible
  low-power actor.

- Harmony assumption: compatible constraint navigation is assumed to
  eliminate conflict.

This framework cannot by itself measure agency, settle disputed
causation, identify moral truth, or guarantee cooperation. Its narrower
contribution is to improve attribution by separating enabling
conditions, causal influence, situated control, and responsibility.

# 12. Applications

Table 3. Applications and safeguards

| **Domain** | **Application** | **Required safeguard** |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Institutions | Trace policy, authorization, enforcement, and correction roles | Do not assign all failure to frontline actors |
| AI systems | Separate model capability from developer, deployer, and operator decisions | Do not anthropomorphize tools or erase delegated control |
| Interpersonal conflict | Distinguish pressure and history from present action | Account for coercion, safety, and unequal exit costs |
| Plural coordination | Coordinate around interfaces and outcomes | Do not force metaphysical or identity agreement |

# 13. Proposed Evaluation Questions

1.  Does the explanation name constraints without attributing intention
    to them?

2.  Are meaningful alternatives, knowledge, coercion, and correction
    capacity specified?

3.  Is responsibility proportional to actual control rather than
    visibility?

4.  Are alignment claims tied to observable intention-action-consequence
    relations?

5.  Can disagreement, exit, and revision occur without identity
    punishment?

# 14. Conclusion

Constraints bound and shape action; agents exercise bounded capacities
within those conditions. Keeping these functions distinct prevents
metaphysical or institutional explanations from silently becoming
substitutes for responsibility. It also prevents responsibility language
from erasing coercion and structural limitation.

Local agency is best understood as traceable, graded authorship.
Alignment is a proposed coherence relation rather than obedience.
Cooperation may emerge from compatible action under shared constraints,
but it remains contingent on power, resources, feedback, and exit.
Properly limited, the framework preserves dignity and correction
capacity without denying either structure or conflict.

## Key Contributions

- An attribution rule separating constraint, causal influence, agency,
  and responsibility.

- A graded account of local authorship that includes distributed
  organizational and technical roles.

- A qualified definition of alignment centered on intention, action,
  consequence, and correction.

- Safeguards against fatalism, context erasure, authority capture,
  metaphysical inflation, and responsibility dumping.

- A practical sequence for mapping agency without treating structural
  constraint as destiny.
